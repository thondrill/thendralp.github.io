Brain-Computer Interface for Art Therapy & Robotic Control

Developing and calibrating a non-invasive brain-computer interface (BCI) system that enables people with limb differences to participate in digital art therapy and operate assistive robotic arms using electroencephalogram (EEG) signals.

My Role Graduate Research Assistant
Laboratory PUSH Studio · DePaul University CDM
Project Type BCI Research, Assistive Technology, Participatory Design
Timeline 2025 – Present
Technologies EEG Signal Processing, Python, Robotic Arm Control, Sensory Feedback

1. Context & Purpose

Creative expression through visual art provides well-documented psychological and therapeutic benefits. However, individuals who have experienced amputations, spinal cord injuries, or congenital limb differences often encounter physical barriers that prevent them from holding traditional brushes, stylus pens, or mouse peripherals.

At PUSH Studio, our research team explores how emerging neural interfaces can dismantle these barriers. We develop non-invasive brain-computer interfaces that translate intention into continuous digital strokes, fluid color mixing, and physical robotic brush movements.

"Currently building a brain-computer interface that lets people with limb differences make art. Emerging technologies must be shaped around human agency, creative self-expression, and dignity."
— Research Mission at PUSH Studio

2. Technical Architecture & Interaction Pipeline

The interaction system pairs a non-invasive multi-channel EEG headset with a custom Python processing pipeline. The raw electrophysiological data is filtered to isolate motor imagery signals corresponding to imagined hand movements and focused attentional states.

Core research and engineering challenges include:

  • Minimizing Electrode Calibration Fatigue: Standard BCI setups require 20 to 45 minutes of tedious calibration before a user can produce an initial input. We developed rapid adaptive calibration routines that reduce onboarding time down to under 5 minutes.
  • Signal Smoothing & Intent Disambiguation: Translating noisy EEG voltage spikes into smooth, intentional artistic strokes without frustrating lag or jitter.
  • Multimodal Sensory Feedback: Providing gentle auditory and visual feedback that confirms when an electrode channel has solid skin contact and when a threshold has been reached.

3. Participatory Design with Target Users

In keeping with our inclusive design philosophy, research sessions are conducted alongside individuals with upper limb differences. Rather than prescribing how an interface should behave, participants guide the mapping of cognitive commands to brush thickness, stroke velocity, and color palette transitions.

Ongoing research directions include integrating assistive robotic arm endpoints that allow participants to paint directly onto physical canvas, closing the loop between neural intentionality and physical medium.
